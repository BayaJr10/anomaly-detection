Is that spike in Nairobi’s air real, or is the sensor broken?

Hewa checks every hourly PM2.5 reading from the city’s low-cost sensors, flags the ones that are unusual for that place and time, and tells you whether neighbouring sensors saw the same thing.

Every flag comes with a likely cause

Low-cost sensors drift, over-read in humid air and sometimes fail outright. A spike only means pollution if it also shows up nearby, so Hewa checks the other sensors in the same neighbourhood during the same hour.

Genuine pollution event

Neighbouring sensors rose too. Something real is in the air, such as burning, traffic or dust.

Goes to environment and public-health officers

Likely sensor fault

Neighbours reported a normal hour, or the reading is beyond what the sensor can physically measure.

Goes to the sensor maintenance team

Can’t be checked yet

No other sensor nearby was reporting. Hewa shows the model’s best guess and the reasons behind the flag.

Goes to an analyst for review

How a reading is judged

From the raw sensor archive to a flag someone can act on, in five steps.

  1. Collect hourly readings

    About 292,000 hourly readings from 53 sensor sites in Nairobi, 2019 to 2026, rebuilt from the sensors.AFRICA archive.

  2. Compare with the usual level

    Each reading is set against the typical PM2.5 for that neighbourhood, local hour and weekday, so a normal rush hour is not an alarm.

  3. Let five detectors vote

    Statistical, density, boundary, clustering and time-series methods each look for something different. A reading is anomalous when at least three agree.

  4. Score and explain it

    A fast model learns that vote and scores new readings in milliseconds, with the top reasons behind every flag in plain language.

  5. Grade and route it

    Severity comes from how unusual and how unhealthy the reading is. Cause comes from neighbouring sensors, and that decides who gets the flag.

How well it does

The model was trained on 2019–2023, tuned on 2024 and tested once on 2025–2026. There are no confirmed labels for Nairobi’s air, so scores measure agreement with the five-detector vote.
